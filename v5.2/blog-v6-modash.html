      The first thing anyone sees on tibba.design is a mountain. It counts up from the valley floor, the camera drops into it, and a thin line climbs to the summit.

      People ask us how it was made, so here’s the honest answer: a year of sketches, one stubborn generator, and a lot of contour lines. We’ve written it up as the eight steps we actually took. You don’t need our code to follow them, just a way to sample heights and draw lines.

      The 8 steps at a glance

        1Pick a symbol you can own

        Tibba means a small hill. We have always described our work as climbing with a client: base camp, the ascent, the summit, and then the next peak. A mountain was the obvious symbol, which is exactly why it was hard. Obvious symbols go generic fast.

        So we set ourselves one rule: no stock peaks, no low-poly triangles, no snowy photograph. The mountain had to be ours, drawn in the same language as the rest of the studio, and it had to move like it was being surveyed rather than rendered.

        “We didn’t want a picture of a mountain. We wanted a map of one, that you could walk.”

        Use contour lines. Each one is a single height, and the spacing between them tells you how steep the ground is. Put enough of them together and a flat screen suddenly has a slope.

        2Build a generator, not a model

        Every mountain on the site, from the hero’s massif to the four client summits, comes from one generator we call the landform. A landform isn’t a model. It’s a recipe: a handful of parts summed together, each with a height, a spread and a sharpness.

        We tuned it in a small internal tool, the Mountains lab, with brushes for adding height, erosion passes, and a button that sends a preset straight into the hero.

        Most of the work is saying no. A ridge that looks dramatic in the lab often looks busy behind a headline. Judge every preset where it will actually sit.

        3Cut character into the cone

        One part on its own is a smooth cone, and nobody believes a cone. The character comes from what you cut into it:

        • Domain warp pushes the ground sideways before it’s measured, so ridgelines bend instead of running straight.
        • Spurs are long ridges that run down from the summit, like the fingers of a hand.
        • Flutes are the narrow gullies between them, where rain would run off.

        4Turn heights into contour lines

        The landform gives you a height at any point. To draw it, sample those heights on a grid and run marching squares over it:

        • For every cell, check which corners are above a given height.
        • Draw the short segment where the line must cross.
        • Stitch the segments together and you get one contour. Repeat for every height and you get the mountain.
        // one contour at height h, over a grid of samples
for (let y = 0; y < rows; y++) for (let x = 0; x < cols; x++) {
  const c = (z[y][x] > h) | (z[y][x+1] > h) << 1
          | (z[y+1][x+1] > h) << 2 | (z[y+1][x] > h) << 3;
  if (c && c !== 15) segments.push(...edge(c, x, y, h));
}

        Draw ribbons, not lines. On our screen each contour is a thin strip of triangles drawn by a shader, which lets us vary its width with the slope and fade it with distance without ever leaving the GPU.

        5Light it, then ink it

        Our mountain lives in two states. In the loader it’s lit: the ribbons catch a soft light from above, the way a relief map does. As you scroll, it morphs to ink, flat lines on black, so it can sit behind text without fighting it.

        Two small effects do most of the work of making it feel real:

        • Aerial fade dims the lines as they get further away, the way distant hills go pale.
        • Pointer parallax moves the camera a few pixels with your cursor, which is enough to make the ground feel like it has depth.

        6Draw a trail that walks switchbacks

        The trail was the last piece, and the one we redrew the most. Our first version ran straight up the slope. It looked like a crack, not a path. Real trails on steep ground zig-zag: long traverses across the face, a tight turn, and back.

        How to write the route

        • Write it around the summit: the distance from the top shrinks steadily as you climb.
        • Swing the angle back and forth across the face.
        • Use a triangle wave with rounded corners, not a sine. A sine spends as long turning as walking and reads as a squiggle; a rounded triangle gives straight legs and hairpin turns.
        “A path that repeats perfectly reads as drawn, not walked.”

        Add a little wobble. A low-frequency wobble in both distance and angle means no two legs of the trail are the same length. Ours draws on as you arrive and finishes on the studio’s mark at the summit.

        7Reuse the range for every summit

        Our client summits are part of the same heightfield as the hero, which is why the home page’s mountain and the Our Work section look like one range seen from two places. Each summit is coloured for its client, and when you reach it, its own trail climbs from the trailhead to the top.

        The same summits also leave the browser. For thumbnails and print, we project the heightfield from the hero’s camera, remove the lines hidden behind the slopes, and fit smooth curves through what’s left. Every illustration is the real mountain, not a trace of it.

        8Ship a film to phones

        A live WebGL scene is a lot to ask of a phone on a train. On small screens we play a short, pre-rendered film of the same mountain instead, made from the scene itself. It looks identical, loads in a fraction of the time and leaves the battery alone.

        Respect reduced motion. If someone has asked their device for less motion, keep the mountain still. The contours tell the story without the camera moving at all.

        FAQ

        Is the mountain a 3D model?

        No. It’s a landform: a recipe of parts summed into a height at any point, drawn as contour lines.

        Are the case-study illustrations drawn by hand?

        No. They’re projected from the same heightfield as the hero, with hidden lines removed and smooth curves fitted through what’s left.

        Why does the phone version look the same?

        Because it is the same scene, recorded once and played as a film, so phones don’t have to run WebGL.

        What we learned

        Build the system, not the picture. Because every mountain comes from one generator, a change to the landform shows up everywhere at once: the hero, the summits, the thumbnails and the share card. The hours we spent on the generator paid for every illustration we didn’t have to draw.

        And restraint is a feature. Most of our best decisions were removals: fewer contours, a single accent colour, one trail instead of five. A mountain only needs one path to the top.
