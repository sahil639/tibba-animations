How we made the Tibba mountain

Tibba Studio 8 min readNov 26, 2026
    Mode:

    The first thing anyone sees on tibba.design is a mountain. It counts up from the valley floor, the camera drops into it, and a thin orange line climbs to the summit. This is the story of how it got there: a year of sketches, one stubborn generator, and a lot of contour lines.

    Why a mountain

    Tibba means a small hill. We have always described our work as climbing with a client: base camp, the ascent, the summit, and then the next peak. A mountain was the obvious symbol, which is exactly why it was hard. Obvious symbols go generic fast.

    So we set ourselves one rule: no stock peaks, no low-poly triangles, no snowy photograph. The mountain had to be ours, drawn in the same language as the rest of the studio, and it had to move like it was being surveyed rather than rendered.

    “We didn’t want a picture of a mountain. We wanted a map of one, that you could walk.”

    Maps gave us the language. Topographic contour lines are honest: each one is a single height, and the spacing between them tells you how steep the ground is. Put enough of them together and a flat screen suddenly has a slope.

    Building the landform

    Every mountain on the site, from the hero’s massif to the four client summits, comes from one generator we call the landform. A landform isn’t a model. It’s a recipe: a handful of parts summed together, each with a height, a spread and a sharpness.

    One part on its own is a smooth cone, and nobody believes a cone. The character comes from what we cut into it:

    • Domain warp pushes the ground sideways before it’s measured, so ridgelines bend instead of running straight.
    • Spurs are long ridges that run down from the summit, like the fingers of a hand.
    • Flutes are the narrow gullies between them, where rain would run off.

    We tuned all of it in a small internal tool, the Mountains lab, with brushes for adding height, erosion passes, and a button that sends a preset straight into the hero. Most of the work was saying no: a ridge that looked dramatic in the lab often looked busy behind a headline.

    From heightfield to contour lines

    The landform gives us a height at any point. To draw it, we sample those heights on a grid and run marching squares over it: for every cell, we check which corners are above a given height and draw the short segment where the line must cross. Stitch the segments together and you get one contour. Repeat for every height and you get the mountain.

    // one contour at height h, over a grid of samples
for (let y = 0; y < rows; y++) for (let x = 0; x < cols; x++) {
  const c = (z[y][x] > h) | (z[y][x+1] > h) << 1
          | (z[y+1][x+1] > h) << 2 | (z[y+1][x] > h) << 3;
  if (c && c !== 15) segments.push(...edge(c, x, y, h));
}

    On screen the lines aren’t lines at all. Each one is a thin ribbon, a strip of triangles drawn by a shader, which lets us vary its width with the slope and fade it with distance without ever leaving the GPU.

    Ink, light and air

    The mountain lives in two states. In the loader it’s lit: the ribbons catch a soft light from above, the way a relief map does. As you scroll, it morphs to ink, flat lines on black, so it can sit behind text without fighting it.

    Two small effects do most of the work of making it feel real. Aerial fade dims the lines as they get further away, the way distant hills go pale. And a little pointer parallax moves the camera a few pixels with your cursor, which is enough to make the ground feel like it has depth.

    Walking the switchbacks

    The orange trail was the last piece, and the one we redrew the most. Our first version ran straight up the slope. It looked like a crack, not a path. Real trails on steep ground zig-zag: long traverses across the face, a tight turn, and back.

    So the route is written around the summit. The distance from the top shrinks steadily as you climb, while the angle swings back and forth across the face. The swing is a triangle wave with rounded corners rather than a sine: a sine spends as long turning as walking and reads as a squiggle, while a rounded triangle gives straight legs and hairpin turns.

    “A path that repeats perfectly reads as drawn, not walked.”

    That’s why the last trick is a little low-frequency wobble in both distance and angle, so no two legs of the trail are the same length. The trail draws on as you arrive and finishes on the studio’s mark at the summit.

    Four more summits

    The client summits are part of the same heightfield as the hero, which is why the home page’s mountain and the Our Work section look like one range seen from two places. Each summit is coloured for its client, and when you reach it, its own trail climbs from the trailhead to the top.

    The same summits also leave the browser. For thumbnails and print, we project the heightfield from the hero’s camera, remove the lines hidden behind the slopes, and fit smooth curves through what’s left. Every illustration is the real mountain, not a trace of it.

    Making it light on phones

    A live WebGL scene is a lot to ask of a phone on a train. On small screens we play a short, pre-rendered film of the same mountain instead, made from the scene itself. It looks identical, loads in a fraction of the time and leaves the battery alone.

    And if you’ve asked your device for reduced motion, the mountain stays still. The contours tell the story without the camera moving at all.

    What we learned

    Build the system, not the picture. Because every mountain comes from one generator, a change to the landform shows up everywhere at once: the hero, the summits, the thumbnails and the share card. The hours we spent on the generator paid for every illustration we didn’t have to draw.

    And restraint is a feature. Most of our best decisions were removals: fewer contours, a single accent colour, one trail instead of five. A mountain only needs one path to the top.

    — The Tibba team
