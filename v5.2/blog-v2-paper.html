How we made the Tibba mountain

A map you can walk: one generator, a lot of contour lines, and a trail to the summit.

Why a mountain

The first thing anyone sees on tibba.design is a mountain. It counts up from the valley floor, the camera drops into it, and a thin line climbs to the summit.

Tibba means a small hill. We have always described our work as climbing with a client: base camp, the ascent, the summit, and then the next peak. A mountain was the obvious symbol, which is exactly why it was hard. Obvious symbols go generic fast.

So we set one rule: no stock peaks, no low-poly triangles, no snowy photograph. Maps gave us the language. Contour lines are honest: each one is a single height, and the spacing between them tells you how steep the ground is.

To this end, we set out to build:

  • A mountain drawn in the studio's own language
  • Motion that feels surveyed rather than rendered
  • Contours that give a flat screen a slope

“We didn’t want a picture of a mountain. We wanted a map of one, that you could walk.”

Tibba StudioDesign lead

Building the landform

Every mountain on the site, from the hero’s massif to the four client summits, comes from one generator we call the landform. It isn’t a model. It’s a recipe: a handful of parts summed together, each with a height, a spread and a sharpness.

One part on its own is a smooth cone, and nobody believes a cone. The character comes from what we cut into it: domain warp bends the ridgelines, spurs run down from the summit like fingers, and flutes are the gullies between them.

We tuned it in an internal tool, the Mountains lab. Most of the work was saying no: a ridge that looked dramatic in the lab often looked busy behind a headline.

To this end, we built:

  • One landform generator behind every peak
  • Brushes for height, erosion passes, and one-click presets to the hero

From heights to lines

The landform gives us a height at any point. We sample those heights on a grid and run marching squares: for every cell, we check which corners sit above a height and draw the segment where the line must cross. Stitch the segments and you have one contour.

// one contour at height h, over a grid of samples
for (let y = 0; y < rows; y++) for (let x = 0; x < cols; x++) {
  const c = (z[y][x] > h) | (z[y][x+1] > h) << 1
          | (z[y+1][x+1] > h) << 2 | (z[y+1][x] > h) << 3;
  if (c && c !== 15) segments.push(...edge(c, x, y, h));
}

On screen each line is a thin ribbon drawn by a shader, so we can vary its width with the slope and fade it with distance without leaving the GPU. In the loader the ribbons are lit like a relief map; as you scroll they morph to flat ink so the mountain can sit behind text. Aerial fade and a little pointer parallax do the rest.

To this end, we're building:

  • Lines as shader ribbons, not strokes
  • Two states, lit and ink, that morph on scroll
  • Aerial fade and parallax for depth

“A path that repeats perfectly reads as drawn, not walked.”

Walking the switchbacks

The trail was the last piece and the one we redrew most. Our first version ran straight up the slope and looked like a crack. Real trails on steep ground zig-zag: long traverses, a tight turn, and back.

So the route is written around the summit. The distance from the top shrinks as you climb while the angle swings across the face, as a triangle wave with rounded corners rather than a sine. A slow wobble in both means no two legs are the same length. The trail draws on as you arrive and finishes on our mark.

To this end, the trail has:

  • Straight legs and hairpin turns
  • No two legs alike
  • A finish on the studio's mark

Four more summits, and phones

The client summits share the hero's heightfield, so the home page's mountain and Our Work look like one range seen from two places. Each is coloured for its client. For thumbnails and print, we project the heightfield from the hero's camera, remove hidden lines and fit smooth curves: every illustration is the real mountain.

On phones we play a short film of the same scene instead of live WebGL. It looks identical, loads faster and leaves the battery alone. With reduced motion on, the mountain stays still.

To this end, we're building:

  • One range for the hero and every case study
  • Clean SVG exports from the live scene
  • A light path for phones and for reduced motion

Build the system, not the picture. Because every mountain comes from one generator, a change to the landform shows up everywhere at once. And most of our best decisions were removals: fewer contours, one accent colour, one trail instead of five.

Thanks for climbing this far with us. A mountain only needs one path to the top — we're glad you took it.
